On one machine, things work or crash. Across machines, they partly fail.

A single computer is deterministic: a bug or crash is total. In a distributed system, some parts break while others keep going, and you often can’t even tell which.

Supercomputers

Checkpoint, and restart the whole job on failure.

Internet services

Must stay up. Build a reliable system from unreliable parts, like TCP over lossy IP.

01

Unreliable networks

You sent a request. No reply. Now what?

No reply can mean six different things.

Request lostRequest queuedRemote node crashedRemote node pausedResponse lostResponse delayed

From the sender’s side they all look identical. The only tool you have is a timeout, and even then you don’t know whether the request ran.

Network faults are surprisingly common, even in a single datacenter: the book cites studies with about a dozen network faults per month in a medium-sized datacenter, misconfigured switches, and even sharks biting undersea cables. Your software must handle them, or at least you must know what it does when they happen.

Lab: choosing a timeout

healthy requests wrongly treated as dead
how long a real crash goes unnoticed
Short timeouts declare healthy-but-slow nodes dead, triggering failovers that add load and can cascade. Long timeouts mean real crashes take long to notice. Delays vary because of queueing: in switches, in the OS, in the VM hypervisor, and TCP’s own flow control and retransmits. Good systems measure the distribution and adjust timeouts automatically (Akka, Cassandra’s phi accrual failure detector).

NTP sync can fail silently (firewalls), leap seconds have crashed systems, VMs pause and their clocks jump, and users’ devices can be set to anything. If you depend on synchronized clocks, monitor clock offsets and remove nodes that drift too far.

Last-write-wins by timestamp drops data silently.

Google Spanner reports a confidence interval [earliest, latest] instead and waits out the uncertainty before committing.

Logical clocks (counters, version vectors) are the safer way to order events. Spanner’s TrueTime uses GPS and atomic clocks in each datacenter to keep the uncertainty around 7 ms.

Your process can freeze at any moment.

The thread doesn’t notice it was paused. Real-time systems avoid this with guaranteed scheduling, but that’s expensive. Practical mitigations: treat GC pauses like brief planned outages (drain traffic first), or restart processes before they need a full GC.
03

Knowledge, truth, and lies

A node can’t trust its own judgment.

Fencing tokens make leases safe.

A node that’s been declared dead by a majority must step down, even if it feels fine.

ZooKeeper’s zxid or cversion can serve as fencing tokens. The storage side must check them; the client can’t be trusted to check itself.

Byzantine faults and system models.

Byzantine faults

Nodes that lie. Relevant for aerospace and blockchains. Inside our own datacenter we generally assume nodes are honest but unreliable.

Cheap defences

Checksums against corrupted packets, input validation, multiple NTP servers.

Safety vs liveness

Safety: nothing bad ever happens (no duplicate fencing tokens). Liveness: something good eventually happens (a request gets an answer).

System models: timing can be synchronous, partially synchronous (realistic), or asynchronous; nodes can crash-stop, crash-recover (realistic), or be Byzantine. Algorithms are proven correct within a model; reality sometimes steps outside it.
